Md Tarif Raihan · Ph.D. candidate, Mechanical Engineering · University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee

Which properties of a porous metal wall set its temperature, and how much pumping power does it take to hold it there?

I work on this question in two places where heat loads keep rising: transpiration-cooled turbine and aerospace parts, and liquid cold plates for data-center processors. I build the experiment, write the model, check one against the other, and only then use the model for control.

2023–2025UWM · experiments

Measuring transpiration cooling through sintered metals

Problem
Turbine trailing edges and aerospace hot sections run close to material limits. Transpiration cooling pushes coolant through a porous wall, but the separate effects of wall material and pore structure were not well resolved in test data.
Did
Designed and ran tests in a two-pass square channel (hydraulic diameter 0.14 m) with a porous end wall at Reynolds numbers of 39,000–70,000. Compared sintered SS316L with titanium, sintered powder with wire mesh, and 80 µm with 100 µm mesh pores. Each campaign used sensor calibration, energy-balance checks, repeated runs and uncertainty analysis.
Showed
Titanium plates performed better than SS316L at every Reynolds number tested, and the gain from higher flow flattened between Re 63,000 and 70,000 (data below). GT2025, HT2025, IMECE2025
Also
Co-author on gas-turbine internal-cooling studies of pin-fin and dimpled channels. IJECE 2025, 2026
Left open
The data showed what changed with material and structure, not why. The permeability values used in the first analyses also disagreed with Kozeny–Carman estimates.
2025–2026UWM · NSF CIBERCATSS

Explaining the measurements with physics

Did
Wrote 1-D and 2-D Python solvers that couple local thermal non-equilibrium (LTNE) heat transfer with Darcy–Forchheimer flow. Verified them with mesh refinement, exact-solution cases and independent experiments, and corrected the permeability mismatch along the way.
Showed
Solid thermal conductivity controls the cooling response, pore architecture comes second, and pore diameter matters least. ASME J. Energy Resour. Technol. Part A, 2026
Then
In summer 2026 (NSF CIBERCATSS, mentor Dr. Tian Zhao), built a differentiable inverse model in JAX-FEM to estimate porous-media properties from temperature data. Porosity was identifiable. Permeability was not: with the coolant mass flux prescribed, permeability enters only the momentum balance, not the energy equations. Paper in preparation
Led to
Designing experiments around what the sensors can identify. Recovering permeability needs a pressure-drop measurement alongside temperature.

Current work

Models that a controller can trust, tested on hardware

The data-center test vehicle is where the porous-media models, the transient results and the control idea from my M.Sc. meet. It is under construction, so this section describes the design, not results.

Data-center thermal test vehicle Hardware validation in progress

Schematic drawn from the project description, not the as-built rig.

  • Physics-informed neural-network surrogate of the loop in Python, trained on 10,000 simulated operating points, with four control and monitoring functions and a Streamlit dashboard. It will be checked against the hardware before it is trusted for control.
  • Planned comparison: physics-based versus learned controllers on pumping energy and heat-source temperature, against a conventional feedback baseline.
  • Additively manufactured porous copper cold-plate cartridges (simple-cubic lattice, 60% porosity) with matching permeability test coupons. Trial parts are being sourced.
  • Wrote the rig's safety, calibration and maintenance procedures.

Rotating transpiration-cooling rig Design stage

Design schematic, not to scale. No transpiration tests have been run on this rig yet.

The lab's rotating two-pass channel models the mid-chord cooling passage of a turbine blade, but its solid heated wall cannot pass coolant. The redesign replaces that wall with a porous titanium plate heated without contact, catches all the air that passes through it in a sealed hood, and measures pressure drop and blowing ratio while spinning.

The aim is first data on how Coriolis and buoyancy effects change transpiration cooling in a rotating channel. We found no such rig in the papers we reviewed. The data will also test the LTNE solvers.

Where the work goes next

One question, three lines of work

What can we learn about heat exchange inside engineered porous media from measurements we can actually make, and how should that change design and control?

Thrust 1 · closures

Identifiable LTNE closures for sintered and printed metals

Most interstitial heat-transfer correlations come from packed beds and metal foams. A transient test cell with step and periodic heat loads, read through differentiable inverse models, will estimate them for sintered and printed plates with confidence bounds. An NSF CBET proposal on this thrust is drafted.

Thrust 2 · design and control

Graded porous cold plates at equal pumping power

How much does grading lower peak temperature at fixed pumping power, and can a physics-based surrogate cut pump energy under changing load without breaking the temperature limit? Results will be reported as measured thermal resistance versus pumping power.

Thrust 3 · systems

Recovering low-grade heat from liquid-cooled systems

Warm-water cooling raises coolant return temperature. Combining cold-plate data with heat-pump and heat-exchanger models and plant-assessment experience, this thrust asks when that heat can serve process or building heating, and which controls make recovery reliable.

I have not yet worked on two-phase flow. I would add phase change in porous structures through collaboration, and only after the single-phase results are solid.
